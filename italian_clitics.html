How to Use This App

What this is

A drill for Italian clitic pronouns — the little pronouns (mi, ti, lo, la, gli, le, ci, vi, si) that replace objects. This is one of the hardest parts of Italian grammar for English speakers, because clitics change both form (which pronoun) and position (before or attached to the verb), and they cascade when combined.

180 cards across four categories: direct object (96), indirect (56), modal double placement (24), and gerund (4).

The hardest part of the language, drilled to automaticity.

The two directions
  • English → Italian — you see the meaning and produce the Italian clitic form. This is production.
  • Italian → English — you see or hear the Italian and identify the meaning. This is comprehension.

Same underlying cards; progress shared. Modal and gerund cards show both valid placements in comprehension, so you see the equivalence directly.

Input methods and prompt modes

Production (English → Italian) has two input methods: Type answer (default) and Honour (self-assess).

Comprehension (Italian → English) has the same two input methods, plus an independent prompt mode:

  • Reading — you see the Italian text. No prompt audio. Fast visual recognition.
  • Listening — you hear the audio, but the Italian text is hidden. Ear training.
  • Both — you see the text and hear the audio together. Reinforcement.

Listening + Honour gives you a pure ear-training drill; Reading + Type gives you a fast recognition drill. Click Listen 🔊 any time to replay.

The Rules
Placement: attached vs preposed

The rule depends on where the verb is:

  • Attached: affirmative imperatives (compralo), infinitives (comprarlo), gerunds (comprandolo).
  • Preposed (before): finite verbs — present, past, future, etc. (lo compro).
  • Either: modals + infinitive (voglio comprarlo = lo voglio comprare) AND negative imperatives (non comprarlo = non lo comprare).

The Italian negative imperative is more flexible than Spanish. Where Spanish only allows no lo compres, Italian accepts both non comprarlo (attached) and non lo comprare (preposed). The deck accepts both.

The pronoun system

Two sets of pronouns: direct objects answer "what?" and indirect objects answer "to/for whom?". They look similar but combine differently.

When combined, indirect goes first: me lo, te la, glieli, ce ne. Never "lo me".

Glielo — the fusion form

Italian's third-person indirect (gli m.sg, le f.sg, gli pl) fuses with the direct clitic into one word: glielo, gliela, glieli, gliele.

So Glielo do means "I give it to him", "I give it to her", OR "I give it to them" — one form, three referents. This is actually simpler than Spanish's le→se rule mechanically, but more ambiguous semantically.

Context disambiguates. The deck drills glielo as one form; the trap note reminds you of the ambiguity.

Consonant doubling

Short imperatives of dare (da'), dire (di'), fare (fa'), stare (sta'), and andare (va') double the initial consonant of an attached clitic:

da' + lo → dallo • da' + mi → dammi • di' + lo → dillo • di' + mi → dimmi • fa' + lo → fallo • va' + ci → vacci

Regular imperatives (compra, vendi, scrivi, etc.) do not double: compralo, vendilo, scrivilo.

No accent marks are needed on clitic-attached forms in Italian — unlike Spanish, which forces accents (cómpralo, dámelo). Italian: compralo, dammelo.

Animate vs inanimate

Italian direct-object pronouns don't distinguish people from things — gender and number alone determine the pronoun. Lo vedo = "I see him" OR "I see it (m)". La vedo = "I see her" OR "I see it (f)".

The deck's prompts mark the grammatical gender with (m) or (f) — not animacy. A prompt like "Buy it (f)" produces comprala — same answer as "Buy her."

Italian doesn't have Spanish's leísmo — there's no regional variant where lo becomes le for masculine animate. Standard Italian uses lo throughout.

Modal double placement

With a conjugated modal verb + infinitive, the clitic can go before the modal or attached to the infinitive. Both are grammatically correct:

  • Voglio comprarlo (attached)
  • Lo voglio comprare (preposed)

Modal cards accept either. Feedback shows both.

False friend — tenere vs avere

Italian tenere means "hold / keep", not "have". Spanish tener does mean "have", but Italian uses avere for that — ho, hai, ha, abbiamo, avete, hanno. Where you'd say tengo la porta aperta ("I'm holding the door open"), you'd say ho la porta aperta ("I have the door open").

The deck drills tenere in its "hold / keep" sense: present lo tengo = "I'm holding it", imperative tienilo = "Hold it!". For the "have" meaning, Italian uses a different construction (ce l'ho, with locative ci) — not covered here.

Trap hints

By default, trap cards show a generic "⚠️ Trap card" badge before you answer. That's enough to know you're on a tricky one without spoiling which trap it is. Turn on Detailed trap hints in the drawer if you want the specific trap named before answering. Feedback always shows the full explanation regardless.

Tu vs Lei — all imperatives are tu

The imperative cards in this deck use the tu (informal) form throughout: Dammelo = "give it to me" to a friend, family member, child, or peer.

The Lei (formal) forms are different: Me lo dia, Glielo dia, etc. — they use third-person subjunctive forms. The deck drills tu only, matching everyday speech. Imperative cards carry a · tu indicator on the category badge.

Voi is universal

Italian's voi (2nd pl) is standard everywhere — unlike Spanish's vosotros, which is Peninsular-only. The ve lo cards are correct in all Italian.

What's not covered
  • Reflexive verbs (lavarsi, mettersi) — separate skill
  • Si impersonale / passivante (si vende, si parla) — different grammar
  • Ne ("of it / of them") — high frequency, distinctly Italian, deferred to v2
  • Locative ci ("there") — deferred to v2
  • Combinations with ne and ci (me ne, ce ne, gliene) — deferred
  • Other tenses (future, past, subjunctive) — same placement rules as present
Suggested Workflow

Start with Direct. Get the basic pronoun forms and preposed/attached distinction automatic.

Then Indirect. The full pronoun system (mi, ti, gli/le, ci, vi) across anchor verbs dare and dire.

Then traps only. Filter to trap cards (doubling + glielo + dual-placement) and drill them specifically. This is where learners plateau.

Then Modal and Gerund. Add the either/attached placement cards.

Rule: Short imperatives (da', di', fa', sta', va') ending in stressed vowel → double the clitic's initial consonant. Regular imperatives ending in unstressed vowel → no doubling.

No Accents on Attached Forms

Unlike Spanish (cómpralo, dámelo), Italian does not add accent marks when clitics attach: compralo, dammelo, glielo. Stress sits where it naturally falls.

The Traps

Consonant doubling: da'+lo = dallo, di'+mi = dimmi, fa'+glielo = faglielo.
glielo fusion: a lui / a lei / a loro + lo = glielo. One form, three referents. Never "gli lo".
Both placements valid: Non comprarlo = Non lo comprare. Don't comprarlo = Non lo comprare (both correct).
Modal two-clitic: Voglio darmelo = Me lo voglio dare — both valid.
Gerund + clitic: Sto comprandolo = Lo sto comprando — both valid, no accent.

Tenere: hold / keep (NOT "have")

Italian tenere = "hold, keep". Spanish tener = "have" — a false friend. For Italian "have", use avere: ho, hai, ha, abbiamo, avete, hanno.
Present-yo: Lo tengo = "I'm holding it" • Affirmative: Tienilo = "Hold it!" • Negative: Non tenerlo / Non lo tenere = "Don't hold it!"
