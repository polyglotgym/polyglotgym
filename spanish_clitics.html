How to Use This App

What this is

A drill for Spanish clitic pronouns — those little pronouns (me, te, lo, la, le, se, nos, os, los, las) that replace objects. This is one of the hardest parts of Spanish grammar for English speakers, because clitics change both form (which pronoun) and position (before or attached to the verb), and they cascade when combined.

180 cards across four categories: direct object (96), indirect (56), modal double placement (24), and gerund (4).

The hardest part of the language, drilled to automaticity.

The two directions
  • English → Spanish — you see the meaning and produce the Spanish clitic form. This is production.
  • Spanish → English — you see or hear the Spanish and identify the meaning. This is comprehension.

Same underlying cards; progress shared. Modal and gerund cards show both valid placements in comprehension, so you see the equivalence directly.

Input methods and prompt modes

Production (English → Spanish) has two input methods: Type answer (default — type the Spanish form) and Honour (self-assess — reveal the answer, then mark yourself correct or wrong).

Comprehension (Spanish → English) has the same two input methods (type or honour), plus an independent prompt mode:

  • Reading — you see the Spanish text. No prompt audio. Good for fast visual recognition.
  • Listening — you hear the audio, but the Spanish text is hidden. Good for ear training — this is the closest drill to real conversation.
  • Both — you see the text and hear the audio together. Good for reinforcement.

Combined with type vs honour, that's six comprehension configurations. Listening + Honour gives you a pure ear-training drill; Reading + Type gives you a fast recognition drill; the other four sit on the spectrum between.

Click Listen 🔊 at any time to replay the Spanish prompt — useful when the listening mode audio didn't play or you want a second hearing. The Auto-speak Spanish toggle controls answer audio only (played after checking); prompt audio is governed by prompt mode.

About the prompts

Some English prompts read a little stiffly, particularly with decir ("I say it", "I say them"). English itself doesn't take bare direct-object pronouns with say very naturally — you'd usually say "I say that" or "I say those things" rather than "I say them". The prompt is written this way to be unambiguous for the drill: it specifies exactly one verb, one tense, one person, one number, and one gender, which produces exactly one Spanish answer.

The Rules
Placement: attached vs preposed

The rule depends on where the verb is, not what it means:

  • Attached to the verb: affirmative imperatives (cómpralo), infinitives (comprarlo), gerunds (comprándolo).
  • Preposed (before): finite verbs — present, past, future, etc. (lo compro), negative imperatives (no lo compres).
  • Either (for modals + infinitive): quiero comprarlo or lo quiero comprar. Both are correct.
The pronoun system

Two sets of pronouns: direct objects answer "what?" and indirect objects answer "to/for whom?". They look similar but combine differently.

When combined, indirect goes first: me lo, te la, se los. Never "lo me".

Animate vs inanimate

Spanish direct-object pronouns don't distinguish people from things — the gender and number alone determine the pronoun. This is a real difference from English, where we say "I see her" (person) but "I see it" (thing). In Spanish, both are la veo.

The deck's prompts mark the grammatical gender with (m) or (f) — not animacy. So:

  • lo → him / it (m)
  • la → her / it (f)
  • los → them (m people) / them (m things)
  • las → them (f people) / them (f things)

So a prompt like "Hold it (f)" produces tenla — and so would "Hold her." Same answer, same form. The prompt asks you to produce the correct pronoun; the underlying referent's animacy doesn't change the target.

In full sentences Spanish adds a "personal a" when the direct object is a specific person (Veo a María, or La veo a ella with the clitic doubled), but the clitic itself is unaffected. This deck drills the clitic form; the personal a is a sentence-level feature covered by the full-sentence apps.

Leísmo — a regional variant

Standard Spanish uses lo for masculine direct objects (animate or not): Lo veo = "I see him" or "I see it (m)". But in some varieties — most notably in Central and Northern Spain — le is used instead when the referent is a specific male person: Le veo = "I see him". This is called leísmo, and it's the single most widespread non-standard pronoun usage in Spanish.

The Royal Spanish Academy (RAE) accepts le for masculine animate direct objects, but not for inanimate ones and not for feminine referents. So Le veo a Juan is tolerated; *Le veo el libro (for "I see the book") is not, and *Le veo a María (for "I see María") is outright wrong — feminine direct objects keep la.

The deck drills standard Spanish, which means lo throughout. If you spend time in Spain you'll hear le used where the deck says lo, and that's a variant to be aware of rather than a mistake. If you're learning primarily for Latin America, you'll hear standard lo almost everywhere and can treat leísmo as a curiosity.

The inverse also exists: laísmo (using la for feminine indirect objects: *La di el libro for "I gave her the book") and loísmo (using lo for masculine indirect). Both are more restricted than leísmo and are considered non-standard by the RAE even in the varieties where they appear. The deck doesn't drill any of these variants — standard Spanish throughout.

The le → se rule

When the indirect pronoun is le or les and it's combined with a third-person direct object (lo, la, los, las), le/les becomes se: dar + le + lo → dárselo. Never "dárlelo".

Accent rules

Attaching a clitic shifts stress position, sometimes requiring a written accent. The rule:

If adding the clitic pushes stress onto the third syllable from the end or earlier, add an accent.
  • Compra → Cómpralo (stress on cóm, now third-from-end)
  • Da → Dalo (no accent; dá-lo is only 2 syllables, stress stays penultimate)
  • Da + two clitics → Dámelo (accent; now 3 syllables)
  • Dar + me + lo → Dármelo (accent; stress shifts)
Modal double placement

With a conjugated modal verb + infinitive, the clitic can go before the modal or attached to the infinitive. Both are grammatically correct:

  • Quiero comprarlo (attached)
  • Lo quiero comprar (preposed)

Modal cards accept either. Feedback shows both.

Tener's dual meaning

Tener means "have" in present (lo tengo = I have it) but as an affirmative imperative, ten means "hold" (tenlo = hold it). Present-yo prompts use "have"; imperative prompts use "hold".

Trap hints

By default, trap cards show a generic "⚠️ Trap card" badge before you answer. That's enough to know you're on a tricky one without spoiling which trap it is — "le→se" in particular would give the answer away. Turn on Detailed trap hints in the drawer if you want the specific trap named before answering. Feedback always shows the full explanation regardless of this setting.

Tú vs usted — all imperatives are tú

The imperative cards in this deck use the tú (informal) form throughout. Dámelo is "give it to me" to a friend, family member, child, or peer. This is the everyday form you'll hear constantly in Spain and increasingly in Latin America.

The usted (formal) forms are different: you'd say Démelo to a stranger, elder, or someone in a formal position, and No me lo dé for the negative. Similarly with mandar: tú No me lo mandes vs usted No me lo mande. The -s ending is the tú marker in negative imperatives.

The deck drills tú only, for three reasons:

  • tú is what most learners use most of the time
  • usted uses third-person subjunctive forms that are irregular across many verbs (dé, esté, sea, vaya, etc.), effectively doubling the verb-set complexity
  • usted forms are more recognisable once you know tú — the shift is learnable in a few minutes and rarely needs drilling

Imperative cards carry a · tú indicator on the category badge so you always know what register you're drilling. Non-imperative cards (present-yo, modal, gerund) are register-neutral: lo compro is "I buy it" whether you're addressing a friend or a stranger.

Os (Spain only)

The os pronoun ("you all" indirect) is Peninsular Spanish. In Latin America it's largely replaced by les, los, or ustedes structures. The card is correct; just noting the regional variation.

What's not covered
  • Reflexive verbs (lavarse, ponerse) — separate skill
  • Se impersonal / passive (se vende, se habla) — different grammar
  • Irregular imperatives (hazlo, velo) — will be v2
  • Vosotros commands (daoslo) — rare in practice
  • Other tenses (future, past, subjunctive) — same placement rules as present
Suggested Workflow

Start with Direct. Get the basic pronoun forms and preposed/attached distinction automatic.

Then Indirect. The full pronoun system (me, te, se, nos, os) across anchor verbs dar and decir.

Then traps only. Filter to trap cards (accents + le→se) and drill them specifically. This is where learners plateau.

Then Modal and Gerund. Add the either/attached placement cards. Note that some clitics shift accent when attached to infinitive/gerund.

The Traps

Accent + imperative: Cómpralo, véndelo, escríbelo, dámelo, díselo, pónmelo, ténmelo. Stress shifts when clitics attach.
No accent, short imperative: Dalo, dala, dalos, dalas, dilo, dila, ponlo, tenlo. Only 2 syllables; stress stays penultimate.
le → se: Dárselo, díselo, mándaselo, tráeselo, véndeselo — never "lelo" or "lela".
Modal two-clitic: Quiero dártelo / Te lo quiero dar — both valid, both accented if attached.
Gerund + clitic: Estoy comprándolo / Lo estoy comprando — accent always required on the attached form.

Tener: have vs hold

Tener = "have" in present tense. Its affirmative tú imperative ten means "hold".
Present-yo: Lo tengo = "I have it" • Affirmative: Tenlo = "Hold it!" • Negative: No lo tengas = "Don't hold it!"
