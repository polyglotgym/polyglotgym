How to Use This App

What this is

A high-intensity drill tool for one thing: Portuguese subjunctive conjugation. The subjunctive is a mood — it expresses doubt, emotion, desire, hypotheticals, and things that haven't yet happened. It's the biggest remaining hurdle after mastering the indicative, and where intermediate learners most often plateau.

This app gives you 26 verbs × 6 pronouns × 5 tenses = 780 cards. It drills form production — same principle as the base Portuguese app, applied to the subjunctive family.

Think of this app as the second act after the base verb terminal.

What this app is not

It drills how to form the subjunctive, not when to use it. Those are separate skills. You can produce fale perfectly and still say espero que ele vem where you need espero que ele venha. Sentence-level trigger recognition is a different drill — see the Conjugation Tables tab for a trigger-phrase reference.

The Five Tenses
Presente do Subjuntivo

The workhorse. Used for present or future uncertainty, wishes, emotion, and impersonal expressions: Espero que fales ("I hope you speak"). Forms from the 1st-singular present indicative, minus -o, plus "opposite-vowel" endings: -AR verbs take e-endings (fale), -ER/-IR verbs take a-endings (coma, parta). This single derivation handles every regular verb and the whole tenho → tenha family.

Pretérito Imperfeito do Subjuntivo

Used for past uncertainty, hypotheticals, and polite softening: Se falasses... ("If you spoke..."). Forms from the 3rd-person-plural preterite stem + -sse endings: falaram → falasse, tiveram → tivesse, fizeram → fizesse. Once you know a verb's preterite 3pl, this is mechanical. Note the -sse vs Spanish -ra: this is one of the biggest Spanish-Portuguese divergences, and worth deliberate practice if you speak Spanish.

Futuro do Subjuntivo

The Portuguese-specific tense. Used after quando, se, assim que, enquanto, logo que, depois que — when referring to future: Quando eu chegar, falo contigo ("When I arrive, I'll talk to you"). Also from the 3pl preterite stem, plus personal-infinitive endings: falaram → falar, falares, falar, falarmos, falardes, falarem. Full section below.

Pretérito Perfeito Composto do Subjuntivo

tenha/tenhas/etc. + past participle: Espero que tenhas comido ("I hope you've eaten"). Used for past events with present relevance in a subjunctive clause. Participle is invariable (-o always).

Pretérito Mais-que-Perfeito Composto do Subjuntivo

tivesse/tivesses/etc. + past participle: Se tivesse sabido... ("If I had known..."). Used for counterfactual past scenarios. Highest tier — on by default but you may want to disable it while starting out.

The Portuguese Future Subjunctive

If you speak Spanish, this is the tense to slow down and study. Spanish does not have a productive future subjunctive — the forms exist but only in archaic legal language (fuere). Spanish speakers instinctively use the present subjunctive (cuando llegue) or the indicative (si viene) for future-reference clauses. In Portuguese, both are wrong: you must use the future subjunctive.

Triggers: quando, se, assim que, enquanto, logo que, depois que, sempre que — when the clause refers to future or uncertain time.

  • Quando chegares, telefona-me. — When you arrive, call me.
  • Se tiveres tempo, vem. — If you have time, come.
  • Assim que pudermos, saímos. — As soon as we can, we'll leave.

How to form it: take the 3rd-person-plural preterite, strip -am, add personal-infinitive endings (∅, -es, ∅, -mos, -des, -em). falaram → falar, falares, falar, falarmos, falardes, falarem. tiveram → tiver, tiveres, tiver, tivermos, tiverdes, tiverem.

Shortcut: for regular verbs, the future subjunctive is identical to the personal infinitive (infinitive + person endings). This is why falar (eu future subj) and falar (personal infinitive) look identical — they are.

Watch out: the future subjunctive of ver (vir, vires, vir, virmos, virdes, virem) collides with the infinitive of vir. Context distinguishes.

The Verb Classes

The six class tags in the drawer teach you how each verb's subjunctive is formed:

AR / ER / IRFully regular verbs. Present subjunctive: opposite-vowel endings from the infinitive stem. Imperfect: 3pl preterite stem + -sse. Future: infinitive + personal endings. These are your baseline.
STEM-IR stem-changing verbs (servir, preferir, dormir, sentir). In the present subjunctive, the changed stem applies to all six persons — sirva, sirvas, sirva, sirvamos, sirvais, sirvam. This is simpler than you might expect from Spanish's indicative "boot" pattern. Imperfect and future are regular.
irr-yoVerbs whose present subjunctive derives from the 1st-singular present indicative minus -o: tenho → tenha, faço → faça, digo → diga, venho → venha, posso → possa, vejo → veja. This is analogous to Spanish's "-go" family (tengo → tenga), but the 1sg forms differ. Imperfect and future subjunctives derive from the 3rd-plural preterite, not the yo-form: tiveram → tivesse / tiver. The class label refers specifically to the present-subjunctive derivation.
IRREGVerbs whose subjunctive is not derivable by either rule: ser (seja), estar (esteja), ir (vá), querer (queira), saber (saiba), dar (dê). Six verbs to memorise. Their imperfect/future still follow the 3pl preterite rule (fosse, for; estivesse, estiver; etc.).
Portuguese vs Spanish Subjunctive

The good news: present subjunctive is very similar in the two languages. Both derive from the yo-form, both take opposite-vowel endings, and for many regular verbs the forms are spelling-identical.

The bad news: the other tenses diverge sharply.

A note on -IR stem-changers

Both languages change the stem in all six persons of the present subjunctive — the "boot" restriction belongs to the present indicative, not the subjunctive. The difference is in the shape of the change:

VerbPortuguese (uniform)Spanish (split)
servirsirva, sirvas, sirva, sirvamos, sirvais, sirvamsirva, sirvas, sirva, sirvamos, sirváis, sirvan
dormirdurma, durmas, durma, durmamos, durmais, durmamduerma, duermas, duerma, durmamos, durmáis, duerman
sentirsinta, sintas, sinta, sintamos, sintais, sintamsienta, sientas, sienta, sintamos, sintáis, sientan

Portuguese uses a single shifted stem throughout. Spanish splits: diphthong (ue/ie) in the boot forms, single vowel (u/i) in nós/vós. That's why servir matches across five of six persons (uniform sirv-), but dormir and sentir only match on nós/vós.

Where the Spanish Diff Filter Works

The "Hide cards identical to Spanish" toggle compares each Portuguese form to its Spanish equivalent (in the current accent mode). It operates on the present subjunctive only, and the payoff varies by verb:

  • Regular -AR, -ER, -IR verbs: roughly 5 of 6 cards hidden per verb — the two paradigms converge closely.
  • Servir: 5 of 6 hidden (uniform sirv- in both languages).
  • Preferir, Dormir, Sentir: 2 of 6 hidden (only nós/vós match, because Spanish uses the split stem).
  • irr-yo and Irregular verbs: almost nothing hidden — different stems (tenha vs tenga, faça vs haga, seja vs sea).
  • Dar: 5 hidden in lenient mode, 2 in strict (accented dê/dês/dé and deem/den differ).

In the imperfect, future, and compound tenses, nothing is hidden — Portuguese -sse, the personal-infinitive future, and ter have no Spanish cognates to match against. That's pedagogically honest: those tenses are the ones you actually need to learn.

After each answer, the feedback shows 🇪🇸 Spanish: X (=) or (≠) — confirming in one glance whether the form matches your Spanish mental model or not.

Accent handling

The subjunctive has accent-bearing forms: falássemos, comêssemos, tivéssemos (imperfect nós); faleis, comais, tenhais (present vós); dê, dês (Dar present). Lenient mode forgives them; strict requires them.

Suggested Workflow

Beginners to subjunctive. Start with Present Subjunctive only (uncheck the other four tenses). Filter to AR + ER + IR regular verbs. Get the basic endings automatic before adding stem-changers or irregulars.

Add the irr-yo family. Turn on the irr-yo class. The pattern is uniform for the present (1sg present minus -o + endings), so if you know tenho → tenha, you can extend to faço, digo, venho, posso, vejo.

Add the six irregular verbs. Ser, estar, ir, querer, saber, dar. Must be memorised. Do them one at a time.

Add stem-changers. Pay attention to Dormir and Sentir — the present subjunctive durma / sinta (uniform shifted stem) is different from the indicative durmo / sinto.

Add Imperfect Subjunctive. Nearly free once the preterite is solid — just 3pl preterite stem + -sse. Watch the irregular preterites (tiveram → tivesse, fizeram → fizesse, foram → fosse).

Add Future Subjunctive. Same stem as imperfect, just different endings. Focus on the trigger contexts (quando, se, assim que) — recognising when to reach for it matters as much as producing it.

Add compound tenses. Present Perfect Subjunctive is a natural next step. Pluperfect is Tier 3 — turn it off while you're starting out if 780 cards feels heavy.

Every third or fourth session. Hit Drill Pending in the Reviews tab.

When you're tired. Turn on 🎮 Play Mode. Nothing gets recorded.

Final Note

Why ter, not haver? The compound subjunctive tenses use ter + participle (tenha falado, tivesse falado), which is standard in both European and Brazilian Portuguese. The haver subjunctive (haja) survives mostly in fixed expressions (haja vista, haja o que houver) and is not the auxiliary you need for fluency.

On vós: The deck includes the vós forms (faleis, comais, partais). In contemporary speech — especially Brazilian Portuguese — vós is archaic or restricted to literature, liturgy, and a few regional dialects. It's included for paradigm completeness. If you're learning Brazilian Portuguese, mentally map vós → vocês (3pl forms) and use the filters to skip these pronouns.

Not covered: the -se variant of the imperfect subjunctive (falasse is drilled; falasse with -sse is the only variant in Portuguese — no analog to Spanish -ra/-se distinction here). Also not covered: the future subjunctive with se in counterfactual (imperfect subj) contexts, the imperative, and sentence-level trigger selection (a separate skill and app).

One collision worth knowing: ser and ir share the imperfect (fosse) and future (for) subjunctives because both verbs have the same 3rd-plural preterite (foram). Context always disambiguates.
