How to Use This App

What this is

A high-intensity drill tool for one thing: Italian subjunctive conjugation. The subjunctive is a mood — it expresses doubt, emotion, desire, hypotheticals, and things that haven't happened. It's the biggest remaining hurdle after mastering the indicative, and where intermediate learners most often plateau.

This app gives you 24 verbs × 6 pronouns × 4 tenses = 576 cards (432 with the trapassato tier off). It drills form production — the same principle as the base Italian app, applied to the subjunctive family.

The second act after the base verb terminal.

What this app is not

It drills how to form the subjunctive, not when to use it. You can produce parli perfectly and still use the indicative parli... wait — parli is ambiguous. You can produce che tu parli correctly and still use che tu parli where che parli (indicative) would be correct. Trigger recognition is a separate drill and a separate app. See the Conjugation Tables tab for a reference list.

The Tenses
Congiuntivo Presente

Used for present or future uncertainty: Voglio che tu parli ("I want you to speak"). Formed from the io-form of the present indicative, with "opposite vowel" endings: -ARE takes i/i/i/iamo/iate/ino, -ERE and -IRE take a/a/a/iamo/iate/ano. The -isc class adds the infix in io/tu/lui/loro only.

Congiuntivo Imperfetto

Used for past uncertainty, hypotheticals, and polite softening: Se parlassi... ("If I spoke..."). Endings -ssi/-ssi/-sse/-ssimo/-ste/-ssero attached to the infinitive stem. Only essere is fully irregular (fu- → fossi).

Congiuntivo Passato

Auxiliary in present subjunctive + past participle: Spero che abbia parlato ("I hope he has spoken"). Used for past events with present relevance in a subjunctive clause. Auxiliary selection follows the same rules as the base app: essere for essere, andare, stare, venire, partire; avere for everything else.

Congiuntivo Trapassato

Auxiliary in imperfect subjunctive + past participle: Se avessi saputo... ("If I had known..."). Used for counterfactual past scenarios. Off by default — turn on in Options when you're ready.

The Verb Classes

The five class tags in the drawer:

-ARE / -ERE / -IREFully regular verbs. Present subjunctive uses opposite-vowel endings; imperfect subjunctive uses -ss- endings on the infinitive stem.
-ISCVerbs like capire, finire. Add -isc- in io/tu/lui/loro (capisca), but noi/voi are regular (capiamo, capiate). The alternation is the teaching point.
IrregularThe sixteen verbs (essere, avere, andare, fare, stare, venire, dire, bere, potere, volere, dovere, sapere, dare, uscire) whose subjunctive doesn't follow the regular stem rule. Each must be memorised, though patterns emerge (e.g. -are stems become -ga/-ia/-a in the subjunctive).

Spelling-change verbs. Cercare is the sole example in the deck. To preserve the hard /k/ sound, c becomes ch before e or i: cerchi, cerchiamo, cerchiate, cerchino. Before a, o, u the c is already hard and no h is needed (cercare, cerco, cercavo). The change affects the present subjunctive only; the imperfect is regular (cercassi etc.). Other verbs in this pattern include pagare (paghi), cominciare (cominci), and mangiare (mangi). None are currently in the deck — cercare is the representative.

Italian-Specific Behaviour
io = tu = lui/lei

The three singular forms are identical in the present subjunctive: parli, parli, parli. This is why the pronoun is always shown above the prompt. It's also a genuine feature of Italian, not a quirk to avoid — context disambiguates in real speech.

io = tu in the imperfect subjunctive

A two-way collision this time: parlassi, parlassi are identical, but parlasse (lui/lei) is distinct. So the imperfect subjunctive has 4 distinct forms across 6 persons.

-iamo collides with the indicative

Parliamo, crediamo, dormiamo are the same in both moods. The subjunctive is marked only on the -iate form (parliate, crediate, dormiate). If you see -iamo, you can't tell from form alone whether it's indicative or subjunctive — context decides.

Auxiliary selection & participle agreement

Compound tenses follow the same auxiliary rules as the base app. For essere-aux verbs, the past participle agrees with the subject's gender and number: sia partito / partita / partiti / partite. For avere-aux verbs, the participle stays in -o throughout: abbia parlato. Use the Speaker Gender toggle in the drawer to drill the correct form for your gender.

Lui / lei — one toggle, two genders

The lui / lei column produces a compound form that depends on the referent's gender: sia partito (masculine) or sia partita (feminine). Both are correct; only one is drilled at a time.

The Speaker Gender toggle governs which participle is drilled — it drives both io (your own gender) and lui / lei (the referent's gender). Set to M → masculine participle throughout. Set to F → feminine participle throughout.

The compromise: the toggle serves both roles, so you can't drill "I (male) say that he left" and "I (male) say that she left" within the same session. If you want to drill both, flip the toggle between sessions. The alternative — accepting both participles as correct for every lui/lei essere compound — would remove the drill's ability to test gender agreement, which is exactly what drilling these forms is for.

Modals and auxiliary choice

All three modals (potere, volere, dovere) are tagged avere. That's the default when the modal governs an intransitive or avere-taking infinitive: ho potuto lavorare, ho voluto mangiare.

But when a modal governs an essere-taking infinitive, the compound auxiliary is essere, not avere: sono potuto andare, sono dovuto partire. The rule is that the modal inherits the auxiliary of its infinitive.

This deck drills the modal's own subjunctive forms in isolation — abbia potuto, sia potuto as standalone cards, without an infinitive. The sentence-level rule (modal carries an infinitive, auxiliary switches accordingly) belongs to a full-sentence drill, not this one. If you're drilling potere in context, remember: ho potuto correre but sono potuto andare.

Dovere — debba vs deva

The present subjunctive of dovere has two accepted sets of forms: debba, debba, debba, dobbiamo, dobbiate, debbano (drilled here) and deva, deva, deva, dobbiamo, dobbiate, devano. Both are correct Italian.

Debba is the more frequent form in modern spoken Italian and is what this deck drills. Deva appears in formal writing, older literature, and occasionally in elevated speech. If you encounter deva in a text, recognise it as a valid alternative — it's not a mistake.

The imperfect subjunctive has only one set (dovessi etc.), so no variant there.

Suggested Workflow

Beginners to subjunctive. Start with Presente only (uncheck the others). Filter to regular -ARE / -ERE / -IRE. Get the basic endings automatic.

Add irregulars one at a time. Turn on essere, then avere, then andare, then fare. Each has a distinct pattern worth internalising before adding the next.

Add -ISC. The switch from capisco to capiamo is easy to get wrong; drill it deliberately.

Add Imperfetto. Almost free once the infinitive stem is automatic — just -ss- endings.

Add compound tenses. Passato is Tier 2. Trapassato is Tier 3 — off by default. Turn it on when the earlier tenses feel solid.

Every third or fourth session. Hit Drill Pending in the Reviews tab.

When you're tired. Turn on 🎮 Play Mode. Nothing gets recorded.

Final Note

This app drills the four subjunctive tenses. Not covered: sentence-level trigger selection — deciding whether a clause needs subjunctive at all — which is a separate skill and a separate app's worth of content. Use this to get the forms automatic; use the trigger-phrase reference in the Conjugation Tables to recognise when to reach for them.
